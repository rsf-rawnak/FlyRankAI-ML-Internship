Abstract

FlyRank's editorial teams can review 20–50 pages a week, not the thousands that need a look — so the real question isn't "what's wrong," it's which pages to open first. Working from a 30,000-page, 32-client anonymized slice of FlyRank's production search data — with the underlying 78.8-million-row fact table used separately to verify the data contract and stress-test for leakage — this project compares a transparent, hand-written priority rule against a client-grouped Logistic Regression trained on the identical held-out split. The rule reached Precision@50 = 0.34; the model reached 0.86, a 2.53× lift over a 54.2% base rate, and the honest client-grouped split cost 6 points of precision versus a naive random split that let 31 clients leak across train and test. The output is a reason-coded, four-action priority queue (refresh, refresh & review CTR, monitor, technical check) intended as decision-support for a human reviewer — not a system that acts on its own.

01

Introduction & problem statement

FlyRank's clients don't have a shortage of pages that need attention — they have a shortage of reviewer hours. A content team can look at maybe 20–50 pages a week; the starter slice alone has 13,152 pages (43.8%) already matching a declining-with-demand pattern. Across 32 clients, no reviewer team is opening thousands of pages a week, which is exactly why ranking, not just flagging, is the actual job.

Decision this improves: given a content item's trailing 90-day search and engagement signals, which pages should a reviewer look at first this week, and what should they do with each one — refresh, expand, protect, prune, or monitor? Unit of analysis: one content item at one point in time. Cost of a wrong call: a false positive wastes a reviewer's limited hours on a page that didn't need attention; a false negative lets a genuinely declining page keep losing visibility unnoticed.

Why this needs a model, not a fixed rule: no single signal cleanly separates decliners from non-decliners. The strongest individual correlations with the decline label were word_count (r = 0.090) and days_since_last_update (r = 0.081) — both weak alone. That pattern — several tangled, individually weak signals rather than one dominant one — is exactly what a learned model can pick up on and a single if-statement can't.

02

Data

Primary modeling dataset: content_refresh_anonymized.csv — 30,000 pseudonymized content items across 32 clients, one row per item, snapshotted with trailing-90-day GSC/GA4-derived metrics (impressions, clicks, sessions, average position, CTR, engagement rate, word count, days since last update, content age, trend direction/percentage).

Independent verification against production scale: before building any features, the data contract was verified directly against the full fact_content_daily_performance table (78,835,655 rows, one row per report_date × client_hash_id × content_hash_id) — confirming the grain held with zero duplicate groups, that availability flags are three-valued (TRUE / FALSE / NULL) and must be filtered with IS TRUE rather than a plain equality check, and that a March 2026 feature window and an April 2026 outcome window were both real, bounded, non-empty slices with full client coverage (0 clients missing between the two months).

03

Methodology

Label

is_declining_label = (trend_direction == "down") — named explicitly as a proxy, not an observed future outcome. trend_direction is a bucket computed from the current 90-day window, so a model trained on it learns "which pages look like they're currently declining," not "which pages will decline next." Base rate: 54.2% (16,262 of 30,000 positive).

Baseline: a transparent rule

Built only after two signal checks with real bucket tables, so the rule's weights reflect evidence rather than intuition:

Signal A — Staleness: decline rate by days since last update
0–89d (fresh) · n=20,65551.2%
90–179d (aging) · n=9,17161.1%
180d+ (stale) · n=17447.1%
Verdict: mixed. Predictive only in the 90–179d band — the 180d+ tail reverses and is too thin (n=174) to trust, so the rule weights staleness accordingly rather than treating "older = riskier" as universal.
Signal B — CTR gap among visible, well-positioned pages (pos 1–20, impressions ≥ 100)
High CTR ≥0.30% · n=5,46153.6%
Low CTR <0.30% · n=9,63066.7%
Verdict: confirmed. A clean ~13pt gap in the expected direction, with comparable average position between groups (9.96 vs 8.66) — so it isn't just low CTR standing in for worse position.

Final rule: visibility gates everything (impressions_90d ≥ 250); staleness (≥ 90d) and the CTR gap add risk weight; ties within a risk tier are broken by log-scaled impressions. No fitted weights, no future-window columns, no label-derived inputs.

Model & validation design

Method: Logistic Regression as the readable first upgrade from the rule, with a Random Forest trained for comparison. Features: 18 numeric + 5 categorical columns from the trailing-90-day snapshot — impressions, clicks, sessions, position, CTR, engagement, word count, staleness, content age, plus content type / intent categoricals. Split: 80/20 GroupShuffleSplit on client_id (seed 42) — every client entirely on one side, so the model can't partially memorize a client's own baseline behavior. Train: 23,837 rows / 25 clients. Test: 6,163 rows / 7 clients. Client overlap: 0.

Leakage audit — three independent checks

  1. Future-window trap. On the full warehouse, a March-only feature set scored an honest ROC-AUC of 0.646; adding the very column the label was built from (April impressions) pushed it to a suspicious 1.000 — caught and removed before it reached the model.
  2. Deliberate re-injection. Adding trend_pct — a direct function of the label — back into the final feature set pushed Precision@50 from the honest 0.860 to 1.000 (+0.140), confirming the test harness is sensitive enough to have caught it if it had been left in by mistake.
  3. Split-honesty test. A naive random row-level split scored Precision@50 = 0.92 with 31 clients appearing on both sides of the split. The honest client-grouped split scored 0.86 with zero overlap — a real six-point cost for closing that shortcut.

Reading it: both models clear the baseline, but the simpler model wins outright — Random Forest's extra complexity didn't earn its keep on this split. Logistic Regression delivers a 2.53× lift over the rule.

What the model leans on

Top Logistic Regression coefficients by magnitude: log_impressions_90d (+1.02, the strongest driver — visibility is the volume base that makes any decline observable at all), log_clicks_90d (−0.69, more clicks lowers risk), and has_avg_position (+0.49, not being ranked at all raises risk).

Where it's wrong

The clearest false positives share an almost identical profile — average position ≈ 23, CTR = 0%, engagement = 0% — which the model reads as strong decline risk, and usually it is. These two pages simply hadn't declined yet in this 30-day window. That's a real limit: the model sees correlated risk, not the cause, so a page that looks exactly like a decliner can just not decline yet.

05

Limitations & honest framing

  • The label is a proxy, not a validated outcome. The headline 0.86 is measured against a current-window bucket label, not a confirmed future decline — the separate March→April future-window check (0.646 honest AUC) used a different, smaller feature set and shouldn't be conflated with the headline number.
  • Staleness is non-monotonic. Predictive only in the 90–179 day band; the 180d+ tail is too thin (n=174) to trust as a standalone signal.
  • The rule baseline ties on a single field. All ten of its top-ranked rows in an early review shared the exact same days_since_last_update value, and 6 of those 10 weren't actually in observed decline — a tie-breaking artifact worth knowing about, separate from the model's own numbers.
  • The honest test set is modest. 6,163 rows across 7 clients is a real but limited sample for a single Precision@50 point estimate.
  • This is decision-support, not decision-making. It orders review candidates by evidence; it does not guarantee any individual page's outcome. 52.2% of the catalog is routed to mandatory human review before any action, precisely because confidence is uneven across the ranking.
  • No causal claims. Low CTR and staleness are observed, correlated risk signals — not validated causes of decline — and a refresh is not guaranteed to reverse a decline flag.
